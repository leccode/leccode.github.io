Physics Informed Neural Networks

Published on October 31, 2026
Author: Constantine Lekkos


We live in an era in which data plays a crucial role in our daily lives. That said, data have always been there to help us draw conclusions and make critical decisions. In recent years, we have taken advantage of technology and AI (a term introduced in the 1950s by Alan Turing), developing algorithms and techniques that help us solve problems across a variety of scientific and industrial domains. For example, we can use medical images to predict a patient's disease or likely response to treatment; depending, of course, on the data.

A category of AI that goes a step beyond Machine Learning (ML) is Deep Learning (DL). The core of DL is Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs). ANNs are built from neurons, each of which computes a linear equation. Below is a simple explanation.

A single neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias:

z = Σ (wᵢ · xᵢ) + b, for i = 1 … n [1]

In vector form: z = w · x + b [1]

The output passes through an activation function: a = σ(z) = σ(w · x + b) [1]

When data is hard to obtain, scarce or of poor quality, a problem arises. In some cases, ANNs can still find and build patterns without much reliable data; a challenge scientists are actively trying to solve.

Such a problem can be addressed using physical laws and equations to help identify the real system. In computational science, scientists and engineers use mathematical concepts to simulate phenomena; ANNs can incorporate this paradigm through differential equations.

From this concept, a new category of ANNs arises: Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). They were first formally introduced by M. Raissi, P. Perdikaris, and G. E. Karniadakis. [2]

PINNs solve problems using partial differential equations (PDEs) and physical laws. These laws are embedded directly into the neural network's training process. The loss function - a metric of the result's quality - is designed to minimize the residuals of the equations. [2][3][4]

L = LPDE + LIC + LBC [4]

where:

  • LPDE is the loss due to the PDE residuals.
  • LIC is the loss due to the initial conditions.
  • LBC is the loss due to the boundary conditions.

Below is an infographic that describes PINNs.

How it works

A PINN minimizes a loss function composed of two parts:

  • Data Loss: The standard error between the network's predictions and any available observed data. [5]
  • Physics Loss: The residual of the governing PDE evaluated at collocation points, which must be close to zero. [5]

By combining these two, the network is forced to obey the laws of physics, making it incredibly powerful for solving forward and inverse problems in scientific computing. [5]
